The Discount Trap

How cutting price quietly drains delivery profit

Pau AnalyticsAnalyst reportBurger & Pizza shop · food-ordering apps
Analyst ReportDashboard
⌂ Home

This burger and pizza shop looked healthy on the order screen. It runs close to 1,000 burger orders and about 470 pizza orders a day through the food-ordering apps. The profit underneath told a different story.

On these platforms the app takes about 30% of every sale, and discounts pile on top. Once the money is counted all the way down to what the shop actually keeps, the busy product turns out to be the weaker earner, and the discounts meant to grow the business are quietly draining it.

The shop can lift profit without adding a single hour in the kitchen: fight to be seen instead of buying orders with discounts, cut the discount that is bleeding profit, and build the menu around the dishes that actually earn once the app is paid.

What the numbers say

1. Being seen beats being cheap

The biggest driver of orders is being seen on the app, far ahead of anything the shop does with price. Holding traffic, ads and season steady, visibility pulls on orders about eight times harder than discounting. Weekends add a steady lift, ads and discounts help but only a little, staying in stock matters, and a slow kitchen is the one thing that pushes orders down.

The lesson is to put budget and effort into ranking and presentation before touching price, and to keep the kitchen fast and in stock. The cheapest way to more orders is to be more visible, not more discounted.

2. Pizza is the bigger-order product

A pizza order is worth about RM 18 more than a burger order, and weekends add a little to every order on top of bringing more of them. The menu, not the marketing, sets the ceiling on how big an order can be.

Pizza and premium bundles belong in weekend promotions, when customers already spend more. A burger-led push will not lift the average order; that ceiling is fixed by the menu.

3. The app's cut flips who is winning

The order screen and the profit sheet tell different stories. Before the app takes its cut, burger makes the most money, about RM 14,040 a day against pizza's RM 13,253. After the 30% commission, the ranking flips: pizza clears about RM 6,899 a day and burger only RM 6,477. The commission lands hardest on the busy, lower-priced, discounted burger.

Burger runs about 983 orders a day and pizza about 473, yet pizza earns more than twice as much per order (RM 14.60 against RM 6.60). Being busy is not the same as making money: the burger line works twice as hard for the same reward. When the kitchen is near its limit, shifting the mix toward pizza means more profit for less load.

4. The shop is discounting past the point where it pays

Profit rises as the discount comes down. For both products, the most profitable discount sits at the very bottom of the range the shop has ever tried. Cutting burger's discount from its current RM 8.30 toward the floor is worth about RM 2,260 a day, roughly RM 826,000 a year. Pizza gains too, but far less. Burger is the worst offender: it gives the deepest discount and gets the least back, because each ringgit off buys orders that never cover the money given away.

The direction is clear even if the exact floor needs testing: cut burger's discount hard, trim pizza's more gently, and move that money into visibility. Two dishes are also too costly to discount, the Double Cheeseburger and the Seafood pizza, whose ingredients eat the most of their price; a deep promotion on either pushes it toward a loss.

5. Which items to push, keep, or drop

On the burger menu, the crowd-pleaser and the money-maker are different dishes. The Classic Chicken sells the most but earns the least per plate; the Mushroom Swiss sells the least yet earns the most. Because it sells in such numbers, the Classic Chicken still brings the most total burger profit, so it stays the anchor, while the richer plates sit under-sold.

Pizza is a happier story. The Pepperoni is both the best seller and one of the best earners, so pushing it costs the shop nothing, and the Meat Lovers is the high-margin dish worth lifting off the sells-little corner.

6. What to do first

The through-line is simple: on a platform that skims a third off the top, order count is the wrong scoreboard, and discounts are draining more than they bring in. The priorities below are ranked by impact for the effort.

Method & data

This report draws on a full year of daily records for the two product lines, 730 product-days covering every day of the year and every lever the shop controls: how many people saw the menu, the discount given, ad spend, how often the kitchen was in stock and how fast it cooked, and the day of the week. Two models were built and checked, one explaining how many orders come in each day and one explaining how much each customer spends; the first accounts for 99% of the day-to-day swing in orders and the second for 97% of the swing in order size. Profit is measured all the way down: each dish's ingredient cost from the shop's own menu, then discounts, ad spend and the platform's 30% commission are subtracted, so profit here means the money left after the app is paid.

Conclusion

A burger and pizza shop on the food-ordering apps can look healthy on the order screen and still be leaking profit underneath. The busiest product is not the best earner, deep discounts are draining more than they bring in, and the platform's cut lands hardest on exactly the volume-and-discount habit the shop leans on.

The way forward is to fight for visibility instead of buying orders with discounts, cut the discount that is quietly bleeding profit, and build the business around the dishes that actually earn once the app is paid. The same kitchen, the same traffic and the same menu then produce materially more profit. That is the difference between a shop that is busy and a shop that is paid.
